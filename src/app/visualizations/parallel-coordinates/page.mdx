# Parallel Coordinates & High-Dimensional Geometry

**Parallel Coordinates** provide a lossless geometric visualization for high-dimensional datasets. First formalized by Alfred Inselberg, this technique replaces standard orthogonal Cartesian axes with an array of equidistant, parallel vertical axes. In multi-variable design analysis, parallel coordinates allow users to trace individual design polylines across inputs and outputs, identify multidimensional trade-offs, and apply constraint filters.

<KeyTakeaways title="Quick Guide & Key Takeaways">
- **Duality of Correlations**: Parallel lines between adjacent axes represent strong positive correlation ($r \approx +1$). Lines crossing in an "X" bundle represent inverse relationships ($r \approx -1$).
- **Multi-Dimensional Brushing**: Setting bounding filters along output axes isolates the specific input parameter combinations needed to satisfy those performance criteria.
- **Feasibility Boundaries**: Reveals empty gaps across axes where physical constraints or solver non-convergences preclude feasible designs.
</KeyTakeaways>

## How to Read This Plot

### 1. Axes & Coordinates
- **Vertical Lines**: Each vertical axis represents a single dimension (either an input parameter or output metric).
- **Scale Range**: Every axis is normalized from its minimum value at the bottom to its maximum value at the top.
- **Continuous Polylines**: Each individual polyline traversing from left to right represents one complete design trial.

### 2. Spotting Correlations & Trade-Offs
- **Parallel Line Bundles**: If lines connect two adjacent axes with matching slopes (straight across), the two parameters have strong positive correlation ($r \approx +1$).
- **"X" Crossing Bundles**: When lines crisscross sharply between adjacent axes like an hourglass, an inverse relationship exists ($r \approx -1$). Notice in Figure 1 that between Peak Stress and Efficiency, higher efficiency connects directly to lower stress.

### 3. Using Interactive Brushing for Inverse Design
- **Applying Brushes**: By selecting a target range on a response axis (e.g. Stress $\le 300\text{ MPa}$ shown by the green box), all designs violating the constraint fade to light gray.
- **Tracing Input Corridors**: Follow the surviving colored lines back to the input axes ($x_1$ and $x_2$). This immediately reveals the feasible parameter range needed to achieve the required performance without trial-and-error simulation.

## Mathematical Formulation

Let $\mathbf{z} = (z_1, z_2, \dots, z_K)^T \in \mathbb{R}^K$ represent a composite design and response vector ($K = d + p$). 

In parallel coordinates, the $K$ dimensions are represented by vertical axes $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_K$ spaced at uniform intervals $d_x$:

$$\bar{X}_k = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \mid x = (k-1)d_x, \; y \in \mathbb{R}\}, \quad k \in \{1, \dots, K\}$$

### Point-to-Line Duality Mapping

Each $K$-dimensional observation $\mathbf{z}_i$ is mapped to a piecewise polyline:

$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{z}_i) = \bigcup_{k=1}^{K-1} \left\{ (1 - t) \begin{pmatrix} (k-1)d_x \\ \tilde{z}_{ik} \end{pmatrix} + t \begin{pmatrix} k d_x \\ \tilde{z}_{i, k+1} \end{pmatrix} \;\middle|\; t \in [0, 1] \right\}$$

where $\tilde{z}_{ik} = \frac{z_{ik} - \min(z_{\cdot k})}{\max(z_{\cdot k}) - \min(z_{\cdot k})} \in [0, 1]$.

### The Inselberg Duality Theorem

- A point $P = (a, b)$ in Cartesian $\mathbb{R}^2$ maps to a line $\bar{P}$ in parallel coordinates.
- A line $\ell: y = m x + c$ in Cartesian $\mathbb{R}^2$ maps to a single dual point $\bar{\ell}$:
  $$\bar{\ell} = \left( \frac{d_x}{1 - m}, \; \frac{c}{1 - m} \right)$$

When $m < 0$ (negative slope / inverse correlation), the intersection point lies *between* the two parallel axes, producing the characteristic "X" bundle.
